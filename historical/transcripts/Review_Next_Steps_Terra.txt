Yes. I think I misunderstood the centre of your question in my previous answer. You are asking whether
Connes’s finite geometry is an effective description of an ordinary higher-dimensional Kaluza–Klein
geometry, and whether the KO-dimension six is the surviving trace of a real six-dimensional internal
space.

That is a deeper question than whether two models have the same low-energy gauge group. And the
9 ↔ 10 ↔ 11 pattern makes it more specific: can a ten-dimensional description carry the even KO
structure while the neighbouring descriptions have odd geometric dimension?

I think there is a concrete way to investigate it, and also a sharp obstruction to one overly strong version
of the idea.

Connes himself noticed the ten-dimensional suggestion: in the 2006 model, spacetime has metric
dimension four but KO-dimension 4 + 6 = 10 (mod 8), and he explicitly mentioned effective
compactification as a possible interpretation. Soon afterwards, Fedele Lizzi considered a six-
dimensional internal geometry whose modes would be frozen at low energies. His proposed parent was
itself noncommutative, and he did not derive the complete Standard Model finite geometry. Dolan and
Nash had also investigated finite matrix approximations to compact manifolds and their chiral modes.
Thus the intuition has precedents, but the particular derivation from commutative KK geometry remains
a substantial question. [arxiv.org +2]

The distinction is between a geometric collapse and a low-energy projection. If a continuous field of
C^∗-algebras is commutative at every nonzero parameter, it cannot acquire a noncommutative limiting
fibre merely by a norm-continuous limit: the commutators of continuous sections vanish before the limit
and therefore vanish at it.

But projection onto a finite set of KK modes can generate noncommuting effective operators. Let P
project onto selected internal modes and define

                                          T_f = PfP.

Although f and g commute as functions,

[links, page 5] https://arxiv.org/abs/hep-th/0608226

----- page 6/8 -----
                          [T_f,T_g] = Pg(1 − P)fP − Pf(1 − P)gP,

which need not vanish. The missing intermediate modes produce the commutator.

There is an elementary circle example. Take

                     P : L^2(S^1) ⟶ span{∣−1⟩,∣0⟩,∣1⟩}, S = Pe^{iχ}P.

Then

                               [S,S^†] = ∣1⟩⟨1∣ − ∣−1⟩⟨−1∣ ≠ 0.

Indeed, S and S^† generate M_3(C) on this three-mode space. So a commutative KK coordinate algebra
can produce a finite matrix algebra after spectral compression.

There are two qualifications. First, f ↦ PfP is generally not an algebra homomorphism: this is a new
effective operator algebra, not simply the ordinary limit of C(S^1). Second, on a circle of radius r, the
±1 modes have energies of order 1/r. In the strict collapse r → 0, at fixed energy, only the zero mode
remains and this particular matrix algebra disappears. To retain a nontrivial finite algebra in that limit,
one needs protected degeneracies—for example, suitable twisted Dirac zero modes.

There is an immediate problem with claiming that this already gives a real spectral triple. In the circle
example, complex conjugation J preserves the selected modes but gives JSJ^{−1} = S^†. Thus

                                        [S, JSJ^{−1}] ≠ 0.

The order-zero condition has failed. This is a useful miniature of the real problem: generating matrices
is easier than generating Connes’s algebra, its opposite action, its fermion module and its real
structure together.

It is only an algebraic toy: the three bundle sectors were chosen as input, and the construction does not

The KO issue is subtler than the dimension count. For the finite Standard Model triple, KO-dimension
six specifies the real-structure signs

                     J_F^2 = +1, J_FD_F = D_FJ_F, J_Fγ_F = −γ_FJ_F.

Its internal metric dimension is nevertheless zero. A genuine six-dimensional spin Dirac geometry has
compatible mod-eight signs, so a six-dimensional ancestor is a plausible interpretation. The signs alone
do not prove that interpretation: one would need a correspondence carrying the relevant real spectral
data and index pairings through the reduction.

There is even a useful K-theory check. For the particular candidate X_6 = CP^2 × CP^1,

                                        K^0(X_6) ≅ Z^6.

On the other hand, regarding the Standard Model algebra as a real algebra, its full abstract
complexification is

                     A_F ⊗_R C ≃ (C ⊕ C) ⊕ M_2(C) ⊕ (M_3(C) ⊕ M_3(C)),

whose K_0 is Z^5. Consequently, the entire real algebra C(X_6,R) and the entire A_F cannot be directly
equivalent in real KK-theory: such an equivalence would induce an isomorphism after complexification.

That does not rule out your proposal. An effective reduction can discard sectors, and a Kasparov
correspondence need not be an equivalence. It does show that the right statement is likely to be “a
selected sector of the KK geometry produces the finite model,” rather than “the two whole algebras are
KK-equivalent.”

There is a particularly suggestive geometry for our actual dimensions. Replace the product CP^2 × S^1
by the Hopf bundle

                                     S^1 ⟶ S^5 ⟶ CP^2.

A suitably squashed S^5 has U(3) isometry, hence the Lie algebra su(3) ⊕ u(1). It is also spin, unlike
CP^2 × S^1. We can then start with

                             M_4 × S^5 × S^2 (11 dimensions),

which has the desired gauge Lie algebras at its symmetric product point. From this common ancestor
there are two different collapses:

                                   S^5 × S^2 --(collapse S^2)--> S^5   and   S^5 × S^2 --(collapse Hopf S^1)--> CP^2 × S^2  [commutative-diagram arrows].

The internal dimensions are 7 → 5 along the first arrow and 7 → 6 along the second. With spacetime
included, these are the eleven-, nine- and ten-dimensional candidates. The ten-dimensional candidate
is therefore related to the two odd-dimensional ones, although it is not automatically an intermediate
point on the same one-parameter path. ^{Oxford Academic}

And here your parity worry becomes a precise spectral obstruction. The Hopf spin structure on S^5 →
CP^2 is nonprojectable. For the untwisted Dirac operator, the fibre momenta are half-integral and all
Dirac eigenvalues diverge when that circle collapses. Ammann’s analysis explicitly covers this Hopf
example. The ten-dimensional base exists as a geometric limit, but its fermions do not simply survive as
ordinary zero modes from the eleven-dimensional spin geometry. A compensating twist or another
mechanism would have to be constructed and checked. The Hopf circle also carries the U(1), so its
gauge-field normalization must be followed through the same limit. [numdam.org]

There is another possible literal chain: collapse the rotational circles of S^2 to obtain an interval, then
collapse the interval to a point. This gives dimensions 11 → 10 → 9, but the ten-dimensional space
has boundaries, and its Dirac boundary conditions become part of the KO problem. It is a different ten-
dimensional candidate from CP^2 × S^2.

So your suspicion has a sharpened form: KO-six could be the remnant of a six-dimensional KK sector
selected from an odd-dimensional parent, while the matrix algebra could arise from projection onto
protected internal modes. Neither follows from the dimension count. The demanding part is to make
the projection, the real Kasparov data, the surviving Q, and the fermion spectrum compatible in one
construction.
